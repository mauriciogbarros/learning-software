# Chapter 7 - While you are coding
- Coding is not mechanical.
  - If it were, all the CASE tools that people pinned their hopes on way back in the early 1980s would have replaced programmers long ago.
  - There are decisions to be made every minute - decisions that require careful though and judgement if the resulting program is to enoy a long, accurate, and productive life.
- Not all decisions are even conscious.
- Developers who don't actively think about their code are programming by coincidence.
- While most of the code we write executes quickly, we occasionally develop algorithms that have potential to bog down even the fastest processors.
- Testing is not about finding bugs, it's about getting feedback on your code: aspects of design, the API, coupling, and so on.
  - That means that the major benefits of testing happen when you think about and write the tests, not just when you run them.
  - When you test your own code, you might bring your own biases to the task.
- It's critical that you write code that is readable and easy to reason about.
- The names we choose define the reality we create.

## Topic 37 - Listen to your lizard brain
- Instincts are simple a response to patterns packed into our nonconscious brain.
- As you gain experience as a programmer, you rbrain is laying down layers of tacit knowledge.
  - Things that work, things that don't work, the probable causes of a type of error, all the things you notice throughout your days.
- Whatever their source, instincts share one thing: they have no words.
  - Instincts make you feel, not think.

### Fear of the blank page
- Everyone fears the empty screen.
- There are two problems that cause this, and both have the same solution.
  - There is some kind of doubt lurking just below the surface of perception.
  - You might simply be afraid that you will make a mistake.

### Fighting yourself
- Sometimes code just flies from your brain into the editor.
- Other days, coding feels like walking uphill in mud.
  - Maybe your code is trying to tell you something.
  - Maybe the structure or design is wrong.
  - Whatever ther eason, your lizard brain is sensing feedback from the code, and it is desperately trying to get you to listen.

### How to talk lizard
>Tip 61: Listen to you inner lizard
- First, stop what you are doing.
  - Give yourself a little time and space to let your brain organize itself.
- If that is not working, try externalizing the issue.
  - Make doodles about the code you are writing, or explain it to someone.
- If you are still stuck, it's time for action.
  - Tell your brain that what you are about to do does not matter.
  - We do that by prototyping.

### It's playtime!
- Look for some aspect of the project you want to explore.
- Do the following:
  - 1. Write "I am prototyping" on a sticky note, and stick it on the side of your screen.
  - 2. Remind yourself that prototypes are meant to fail. And remind yourself that prototypes get thrown away, even if they don't fail. There is no downside to doing this.
  - 3. In your empty editor buffer, create a comment describing in one sentence what you want to learn or do.
  - 4. Start coding.
- You will be surprised to find yourself humming along with your music.
  - At this stage, you know what to do.
  - Delete all the prototype code, throw away the sticky note, and fill that empty editor buffer with bright, shiny new code.

### Not just your code
- It applies to the bigger picture as well.
- Listen to your instincts and avoid the problem before it jumps out at you.

## Topic 38 - Programming by coincidence
- As developers, we also work in minefields.
- We should avoid programming by coincidence in favor of programming deliberately.

### How to program by coincidence
#### Accidents of implementation
- You end up relying on undocumented error or boundary conditions.
- For code you write that others will call, the basic principles of modularization and of hiding implementation behind small, well-documented interfaces can all help.
- For routines you call, rely only on documented behavior.

#### Close enough isn't

#### Phantom patterns
- Don't assume it, prove it.

#### Acidents of context
- What are you relying on that isn't guaranteed?
- Finding an answer that happens to fit is not the same as the right answer.

>Tip 62: Don't program by coincidence

#### Implicit assumptions
- Assumptions that aren't based on well-established facts are the bane of all projects.

### How to program deliberately
- Always be aware of what you are doing.
- Can you explain the code, in detail, to a more junior programmer?
- Don't code in the dark. If you are not sure why it works, you won't know why it fails.
- Proceed from a plan.
- Rely only on reliable things.
- Document your assumptions.
- Don't just test your code, but test your assumptions as well.
- Prioritize your effort.
- Don't let existing code dictate future code.

## Topic 39 - Algorithm speed
- There is another kind of estimating that Pragmatic Programmers use almost daily: estimating the resources that algorithms use - time, processor, memory, and so on.

### What do we mean by estimating algorithms?
- Most nontrivial algorithms handle some kind of variable input.
  - Normally, the size of this input will affect the algorithm.
  - Ther larger the input, the longer the running time or the more meory used.

### Big-O Notation
- The Big-O notation, written $O()$, is a mathematical way of dealing with approximations.
  - When we write that a particular sort routine sorts `n` records in $O(n^2)$ time, we are simply saying that the worst-case time taken will vary as the square of `n`.
- The $O()$ notation puts an upper bound on the value of the thing we are measuring (time, memory, and so on).
- The highest-order term will dominate the value as `n` increases, the convention is to remove all low-order terms, and not to bother showing any constant multiplying factors.
  - $O(n^2/2 + 3n)$ is the same as $O(n^2/n)$ is the same as $O(n^2)$
- Big-O is never going to give you actual numbers for time or memory or whatever: it simply tells you how these values will change as the input changes.

### Common sense estimation
- You can estimate the order of many basic algorithms using common sense.
  - Simple loops: likely to be $O(n)$
  - Nested loops: likely to be $O(m * n)$
  - Binary chop: if your algorithm halves the set of things it considers each time around the loop, then it is likely to be logarithmic, $O(log(n))$.
  - Divide and conquer: algorithms that partition their input work on the two halves independently, and then combine the reuslt can be $O(n * log(n))$.
  - Combinatoric: whenever algorithms start looking at the permutation of things, their running times may get out of hand. This is because permutation involve factorials.

### Algorithm speed in practice
- It's unlikely that you will spend much time during your career writing sort routines.
  - The ones in the libraries available to you will probably outperform anything you may write without substantial effort.
- Whenever you find yourself writing a simple loop, you know that you have an $O(n)$ algorithm.
  - If that loop contains an inner loop, then you are looking at $O(m x n)$.
- You should be asking yourself how large these values can get.

>Tip 63: Estimate the order of your algorithms

- There are some approaches you can take to address potential problems.
  - If you have an algorithm that is $O(n^2)$, try to find a divide-and-conquer approach that will take you down to $O(n * log(n))$
- If you are not sure how long your code will take, or how much memory it will use, try running it, varying the input record count or whatever is likely to impact the runtime.
- Also consider just what you are doing in the code itself.
  - A simple $O(n^2)$ loop may well perform beter than a complex $O(n * log(n))$ for smaller values of `n`, particularly if the $O(n * log(n))$ algorithm has an expensive inner loop.

- In the middle of all this theory, don't forget that there are practical considerations as well.
  - Runtime may look like it increases linearly for small input sets. But feed the code millions of records and suddenly the time degrades as the system starts to thrash.
  - Try to cover both the theoretical and practical bases.

>Tip 64: Test your estimates

### Best isn't always best
- You also need to be pragmatic about choosing appropraite algorithms.
  - The fastest one is not always the best for the job.
  - Given a small input set, a straightfoward insertion sort will perform just as well as a quicksort, and will take you less time to write and debug.
  - Also be careful if the algorithm you choose has a high setup cost.
- Also be wayr of *premature optimization*.
  - It's always a good idea to make sure an algorithm really is a bottleneck before investing your precious time trying to improve it.

## Topic 40 - Refactoring
- As a program evolves, it will becomes necessary to rething earlier decisions and rework portions of the code.
  - This process if perfectly natural.
  - Code needs to evolve; it's not a static thing.
- Using construction as the guiding metaphor implies the following steps:
  - 1. An architect draws up blueprints.
  - 2. Contractors dig the foundation, build the superstructure, wire and plumb, and apply finishing touches.
  - 3. The tenants move in and live happily ever after, calling building maintenance to fix any problems.
- Software doesn't quite work that way.
  - Rather than construction, software is more like gardening - it is more organic than concrete.
  - You plant many things in a garden according to an initial plan and conditions.
  - Some thrive, others are destined to end up as compost.
  - You may move plantings relative to each other to take advantage of the interplay of light and shadow, wind and rain.
  - Overgrown plants get split and pruned, and colors that clash may get moved to more aesthetically pleasing locations.
  - You pull weeds, and you fetilize plantings that are in need of some extra help.
  - You constantly monitor the health of the garde, and make adjustments (to the soil, the plants, the layout) as needed.

- Rewriting, reworking, and re-achitecturing code is collectively known as *restructuring*.
  - There is a subset of that activity that has become practiced as *refactoring*.
  - Defined by Martin Fowler as a *disciplined technique for restructuring an existing body of code, altering its internal structure without changing its external behavior*.

- The critical parts of this defition are:
  - 1. The activity is disciplined, not a free-for-all
  - 2. External behavior does not change; this not the time to add features.

- Refactoring is not intended to be a special, high-ceremony, once-in-a-while activity.
  - Instead, refactoring is a day-to-day activity, taking low-risk small steps, more like weeding and raking.
  - It's a targeted, precision approach to help keep the code easy to change.

- In order to guarantee that the external behavior hasn't changed, you need good, automated unit testing that validates the behavior of the code.

### When should you refactor?
- You refactor when you have learned something, when you understand something better than you did last year, yesterday, or even just ten minutes ago.
- Any number of things may cause code to qualify for refactoring:
  - Duplication
  - Nonorthogonal design
  - Outdated knowledge
  - Usage: some features are now more important than previously thought, and "must have" features perhaps weren't.
  - Performance
  - The Tests Pass
- Refactoring your code is really an exercise in *pain management*.

### Real-world complications
>Tip 65: Refactor early, refactor often
- Collateral damage in code can be just as deadly over time.
- Refactoring, as with most things, is easier to do while the issues are small, as an ongoing activity while coding.

### How do you refactor?
- At its heart, refactoring is redesign.
- Martin Fowler offers the following simple tips on how to refactor without doing more harm than good:
  - 1. Don't try to refactor and add functionality at the same time.
  - 2. Make sure you have good tests before you begin refactoring.
  - 3. Take short, deliberate steps: move a field from one class to another, split a method, rename a variable...
